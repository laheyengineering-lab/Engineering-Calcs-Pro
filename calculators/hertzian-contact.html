Hertzian Contact Stress Calculator

Estimate the contact width or radius and the maximum contact pressure for idealized elastic contact between two cylinders (line contact) or two spheres (point contact).

Engineering Theory

Engineering Context

Hertz theory gives the elastic contact area and pressure distribution between curved bodies pressed together. Two cases are supported, and they use different equations: two parallel cylinders contact along a narrow strip (line contact, plane strain), while two spheres contact over a small circle (point contact).

The equivalent radius combines the body curvatures: \(1/R = 1/R_1 + 1/R_2\) for two convex bodies, \(1/R = 1/R_1\) for a body on a flat plate, and \(1/R = 1/R_1 - 1/R_2\) for a body inside a larger concave body.

Governing Equations

\[ \frac{1}{E^*} = \frac{1-\nu_1^2}{E_1} + \frac{1-\nu_2^2}{E_2} \qquad \frac{1}{R} = \frac{1}{R_1} \pm \frac{1}{R_2} \]
\[ \text{Cylinders:}\quad w = \frac{F}{L} \qquad b = \sqrt{\frac{4\,w\,R}{\pi E^*}} \qquad p_{max} = \frac{2w}{\pi b} \]
\[ \text{Spheres:}\quad a = \left(\frac{3 F R}{4 E^*}\right)^{1/3} \qquad p_{max} = \frac{3F}{2\pi a^2} \qquad \delta = \frac{a^2}{R} \]

Variable Definitions

  • \(E_1, E_2\), \(\nu_1, \nu_2\) = Young's modulus and Poisson's ratio of each body
  • \(E^*\) = effective (contact) modulus
  • \(R_1, R_2\) = body radii (half the entered diameters); \(R\) = equivalent radius
  • \(F\) = total normal contact force; \(L\) = contact length; \(w\) = force per unit length
  • \(b\) = contact half-width (cylinders), contact width = \(2b\)
  • \(a\) = contact radius (spheres); \(\delta\) = approach of the body centres
  • \(p_{max}\) = maximum contact pressure at the centre of the contact. The mean pressure is \(\pi p_{max}/4\) (cylinders) or \(2p_{max}/3\) (spheres)

Assumptions & Limitations

  • Linear elastic, isotropic, homogeneous bodies with contact dimensions much smaller than the body radii
  • Frictionless surfaces, normal load only, static loading, smooth surfaces
  • Cylinders: parallel axes, uniform load along L, length much larger than the contact width, no edge or crowning effects
  • Spheres: point contact; a flat plate is treated as a sphere of infinite radius
  • Approximate maximum shear stress (about 0.30 pmax at 0.786 b for cylinders, 0.31 pmax at 0.48 a for spheres) uses the ν ≈ 0.3 coefficients of the references
  • Maximum contact pressure is not a complete fatigue, wear, yielding or service-life assessment

Worked Example

Two spheres: D1 = D2 = 50 mm (equivalent R = 12.5 mm), F = 1000 N, both steel with E = 200 GPa and ν = 0.30.

\[ E^* = \frac{1}{2(1-0.3^2)/200\ \mathrm{GPa}} = 109.89\ \mathrm{GPa} \qquad a = \left(\frac{3(1000)(0.0125)}{4(109.89\times10^9)}\right)^{1/3} = 0.4402\ \mathrm{mm} \]

\[ p_{max} = \frac{3(1000)}{2\pi (0.4402\times10^{-3})^2} = 2464\ \mathrm{MPa} \qquad \delta = \frac{a^2}{R} = 0.0155\ \mathrm{mm} \]

Two cylinders: D1 = D2 = 100 mm (equivalent R = 25 mm), L = 40 mm, F = 20 kN, steel/steel (E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.30): w = 500 N/mm, b = 0.3806 mm, pmax = 836.4 MPa.

Engineering Considerations

  • Contact stresses are highly local and fall off quickly with depth; subsurface shear is often what governs rolling-contact fatigue.
  • A result near or above the material's yield or hardness-based limits means the elastic assumptions no longer hold.
  • Real parts have finite length, crowning, roughness, lubrication and misalignment that change the pressure distribution.
